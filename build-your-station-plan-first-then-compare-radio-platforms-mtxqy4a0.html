Choosing an Online Radio Platform: A Pre-Launch Evaluation Guide

Many people who want to start a radio station put much of their attention into the programming: the music, the personalities and the overall style. That is natural, since programming is what people remember. Even so, the technical layer decides whether those shows actually reach anyone.

This article walks through the questions you should settle ahead of launch day. It is written for broadcasters in the US who are weighing providers and would like a practical method instead of a pile of product names.

Start with a map of your own station

A feature list says very little unless you know what your project actually needs. Before reading a single pricing page, write down the essentials: how much of the day will be live, how many hours should run on autopilot, and where you expect your core audience to be.

A talk show with a single studio needs a stable live feed and easy handoffs between hosts. A playlist-based station, in contrast, relies on programming tools and storage for its library. A school running an internal station might care more about straightforward access than about technical depth. Once this profile exists, each comparison gets faster.

Existing AM and FM broadcasters should add another line to the map: the way the signal gets out of the existing studio. Streaming the same program on the internet usually requires taking the studio feed and sending it to the streaming server. Ask whether the service supports you with the setup, as it is the point where a lot of FM-to-online projects get stuck.

Test the path your audio takes

The first technical question is how the signal gets out of the booth. Check what broadcasting software the provider supports. A healthy sign is compatibility with widely used tools that use the Icecast and Shoutcast protocols, such as butt, RadioCaster, Simplecast and similar programs. That way, your current setup keeps working on day one.

It is also useful when the platform includes its own streaming tool. BRLOGIC, for instance, built its BRLOGIC Broadcaster, a free application that launches a broadcast after you enter your broadcast key. Beyond the software, confirm the formats on offer (MP3 and AAC are standard), the bitrate every plan tier provides, and whether SSL streaming is part of the package, because modern browsers are strict about insecure players.

Look at what happens when nobody is in the studio

Few stations stay live twenty-four hours a day. The hours in between live shows shape how polished your station feels to someone tuning in. This is why the automation layer merits a close look during the evaluation.

Rather than asking only whether there is an Auto DJ, test how it behaves in real use. Are you able to set playlists for specific times? Is it possible for your producer to drop a new track into the schedule with no need for rebuilding every playlist? BRLOGIC describes this as a dynamic queue, and it is the type of detail that spares hours over a month. Check as well how much storage each plan reserves for songs, vignettes and ads.

Think about where listeners will press play

A live feed that nobody knows how to open brings no real value. The second part, then, is listing all the places in which someone might start listening: a website, an embedded player on a partner page, a link-in-bio page on social media, a phone app, a voice assistant or a car dashboard.

Ask what the dashboard will tell you

After the station goes live, decisions about the schedule should rely on evidence, not gut feeling. During the evaluation, check what audience reports the provider provides. Helpful metrics are live listener counts, a geographic breakdown by country, state and city, listening history and a log of what was played.

Two points frequently get overlooked. One is export: reports you can export to PDF help in conversations with sponsors. The second is log export for compliance checks, and this becomes important when a third party asks for a trail of what was broadcast. A service that offers both spares your team plenty of manual work down the road.

Numbers also support schedule decisions over time. When the numbers reveal that a good portion of the audience listens during the morning commute, that slot needs live content. When a region you never targeted starts to show up in the location data, it might be worth creating content for that audience.

Understand what the platform does not cover

This is an issue that first-time broadcasters frequently skip: a hosting platform delivers the audio, yet it does not on its own license the music you broadcast. Even when a provider includes a music archive, read the conditions with care. The BRLOGIC platform, for instance, shows its song archive alongside a note that it does not include copyright licensing.

In the US, royalty reporting is the duty of whoever runs the station, and the rules change with what you play and how you stream it. Talk to a professional ahead of going live. At the same time, keep your playback logs from the first day, as that history makes future paperwork a lot easier.

Think about how new listeners will find you

A polished stream also needs an entry point. Some listeners will type your address straight into the browser, others will tap a shared link in a profile, and a few more will search inside radio directories. All of those routes depend on something different from your provider.

For aggregators, what matters is a playable link in standard formats. Check for a link generator that creates Icecast, PLS and M3U addresses, since these formats are what most directories expect. On social networks, a link-in-bio page that includes a player allows people to press play without having to leave the profile link. For people who type your address, a branded domain makes your brand easy to remember.

Regarding domains, confirm whether it is possible to get a new domain through the provider or link a domain you already have, and whether that domain can point to your links page or your web app. Email accounts on your own domain are another item worth confirming tier by tier, as those are not part of every tier.

Review the security and control options

Day to day, nobody thinks about security settings. Then a login leaks, a guest host holds on to the keys, or a region has to be excluded because of a contract. When it happens, the controls you tested in advance make all the difference.

Good items to look for are multiple broadcast passwords, so each presenter receives a personal login; the option to block a listener, to handle abusive connections; and geographic blocking, when you have rights that cover only certain countries. A short script for the sales conversation

Many providers offer a sales contact to answer questions ahead of signing up. Use that call with concrete questions rather than vague ones. Useful examples: which encoders have you seen with your servers? How does it work if my connection cuts out during a program? What features on my map are part of the package at the level I am looking at, and which ones are on request? Their answers to these questions say more than any product page.

Rehearse the launch before announcing it

A reliable way to confirm a choice is a closed rehearsal. Pick a quiet day, hook up the studio and follow these steps:

  • Broadcast live for an hour and listen on two different devices.
  • Leave the automation running through the night and review the played-audio log in the morning.
  • Load the player on the station site and on a links page.
  • Bring in some trusted listeners and compare the geographic data in the statistics.
  • Send a support request and note how long the answer takes.

If every item above passes, you are prepared to go public. If not, you found the problem before the audience did, which is precisely the purpose.

Questions and answers

Can I start an internet radio station without a studio?

No. To go live, a regular computer connected to the internet and a decent microphone are sufficient to get started. Plenty of stations also rely on automation to remain on air when nobody is live.

Can I keep my current broadcasting software?

Often, yes. Services that support the Icecast and Shoutcast protocols usually accept popular encoders like butt, Oddcast and SamCast. Confirm the compatibility list with your provider.

Does the platform license the music I play?

A streaming service carries your audio, while music licensing stays with the broadcaster. Review the terms of each music library closely and talk to a licensing professional.

Do all plans come with store apps?

Not necessarily. A Progressive Web App can cover phones and computers, whereas apps listed in Google Play and the App Store depend on the tier you choose or are offered on request. Make sure to verify what each tier covers.

How should I choose a plan?

Start from your own needs: live hours, automated hours, audience size and the places where listeners will tune in. After that, check those needs with the plan table.

Is listener location available?

On services that offer location reports, you can. Ask about reports split by country, state and city, together with listening history and report exports.
